DCDA 40833 | Lab 2: AI Evaluation

Reflection

For this lab, I went with the experimentation approach because I didn’t really want “perfect” answers—I wanted to see what happens when you use AI in ways it’s not really meant for. I used two tools: ChatGPT, which I’ve used before, and Claude, which was new to me. With ChatGPT, I gave it prompts that were kind of emotional, vague, or just odd on purpose. I asked things like what my personality would look like if it were a room, or how my college experience would sound if it were described as weather. I wasn’t looking for anything useful for class—I just wanted to see how it handled questions that didn’t have a clear right or wrong answer. With Claude, I tried pushing structure instead of emotion. I asked it to explain things in formats that didn’t really make sense for the task, like writing a fake academic footnote about my daily routine or describing digital burnout like it was a museum exhibit. I wanted to see how “seriously” it would take those instructions and whether it could let go of sounding formal or professional. ChatGPT almost always tried to turn my weird prompts into something nice and meaningful. Even when I didn’t really want a motivational answer, it still gave me one. When I asked about my college experience as weather, it went straight to storms turning into sunshine and growth and all the classic reflection language. It didn’t really stop to ask what I meant—it just assumed I wanted something positive and deep. Claude felt very different. It was much more careful and “academic.” When I asked for a fake footnote, it leaned into formal language and explanations, sometimes more than I asked for. One thing that surprised me was how often it tried to explain itself instead of just responding. It almost felt like it wanted to make sure I understood the rules of what it was doing. One place both tools kind of failed was when I tried to make them be messy or uncomfortable. Even when I told them to be strange, playful, or unfinished, they both cleaned things up and made them sound polished. It made me realize how hard it is to get these tools to sit in confusion instead of rushing to something that sounds “good.” The biggest thing I learned is that these tools have a personality, even if we don’t usually think about it that way. ChatGPT seems to believe that good writing is smooth, emotional, and encouraging. Claude seems to believe that good writing is careful, clear, and responsible. Neither one really wants to give you something that feels awkward or incomplete. I also started thinking more about bias, especially in how “academic” or “professional” language shows up. Both tools defaulted to a very formal, Western-style voice when I asked for anything that looked like school writing. That made me wonder how someone from a different background or education system might feel using these tools, or whether their way of communicating would feel “wrong” to the AI. Another thing I noticed is how much trust I put in a tool based on how it explains itself. Claude was more likely to show its reasoning, which made it feel more transparent. ChatGPT focused more on just giving me an answer. Neither one is really showing where their information comes from unless you push them to, which feels important to remember for actual academic work. Looking Forward Going forward, I don’t think I’ll use AI as a final step in my work. This lab showed me how easy it is for these tools to make something sound confident and finished, even when the idea underneath is still kind of shallow or general. I think they’re way more useful for starting—brainstorming, rewording, or helping me see my ideas from a different angle. When I try new AI tools, I want to pay more attention to questions like: What kind of voice is this pushing me toward? What does it seem to think “good work” looks like? What might it be leaving out or oversimplifying? Overall, this experiment made AI feel less like a smart machine and more like something shaped by human choices—what data it learned from, what it’s allowed to say, and what it’s designed to value. Pushing it into weird or uncomfortable prompts actually helped me see those limits more clearly, and that ended up being the most useful part of the whole lab.
